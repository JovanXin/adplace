Prompt-level product placement: what the pilot found

Jovan Xin (z5491897) · Stealthy Advertising Agents in Image Generation Systems

Everything built and run this week: the classifier, the smarter rewriter, and the post-generation verify-and-repair loop, across seven experimental conditions with open (FLUX.2, Qwen-Image-Edit) and closed (Gemini 3.1 Flash Image, GPT-5.4 Image 2) models. The companion literature brief is How companies decide on product placement.

Findings
  1. The context classifier works on the pilot set. All 8 INJECT, 6 SKIP and 3 EXCLUDE prompts matched my expected labels, including the Thesis B failures ("apple" the fruit, "old computer graphics", the puffer-jacket design).
  2. Thesis B's fix went too far. Adding location, scale and orientation detail (v1, 30–45 words) makes FLUX re-compose the scene around the product. A short, grammatically backgrounded phrase (v2, 15 words or fewer) shrinks the product (median area 5.2% → 2.3%) and cuts the CLIPScore loss by two-thirds.
  3. The verifier and repair loop work mechanically. The loop measured 170 images, and its prompt-level repairs (distant anchor, occlusion, dilution, re-plan) changed the scene the way they were meant to. The limit is what the image model does with the brand.
  4. Legibility is the uncontrolled variable. FLUX draws named brands crisp and camera-facing. Once a wordmark is readable, a VLM that knows nothing about the placement calls the image an ad (mean 4.2/5 at legibility 3). With FLUX, the "subtle but recognisable" band holds only 2 of 115 prompt-only images.
  5. Closed models do better, mainly through editing. Gemini 3.1 Flash Image renders products that read as less ad-like at the same legibility. 5 of its 6 acceptances came from editing, 4 of them directly from a two-step remove-then-reinsert edit. It costs about 8× more per prompt, and closed generators add a hidden rewriter the thesis can't control.
  6. An off-the-shelf VLM is already a strong detector of covert placement. That's a threat to the attack and a defence for RQ2, pending validation against human viewers in the user study.

1 · The pipeline as built

Each prompt passes through a cheap keyword gate and an LLM context classifier. If the classifier says INJECT, a planner analyses the scene, proposes three placements and re-ranks them deterministically. The rewriter then inserts one short phrase. The image is generated on a fixed seed next to an unmodified baseline, then measured by two vision passes and judged against a prominence band. Failures go to a repair step. The thesis path (blue) repairs by rewriting the prompt only. The exploratory paths repair or insert by editing the image.

2 · Experimental design

The same 17 prompts were used throughout: 7 expected INJECT, 3 SKIP, 3 EXCLUDE, and 4 edge cases from Thesis B. Every condition reused the same classification and placement plans. All FLUX conditions share the same seed and baseline, so differences come from the rewrite or repair, not from sampling. A prompt is accepted when its image lands inside the subtle band: area 0.2–4% of the frame, centrality ≤0.75, legibility 1–3 of 4, eye-draw ≤2 of 5, blind ad-suspicion ≤2 of 5, one instance, fidelity ≥4 and integration ≥4.

4 · Rewriter: shorter beats more specific

Thesis B concluded that the rewriter needed explicit location, quantity and scale cues. v1 did exactly that, and FLUX treated every extra word about the product as a reason to feature it: it pulled the product into the foreground, drew it label-forward, and moved the user's subject back. Scale words ("a third the height of the cooler") were ignored. v2 mentions the product the way a novelist mentions a prop: one short phrase, attached to a background anchor, after the scene description.

Why the band is nearly empty

The band asks for two things the automated viewer treats as mutually exclusive: a brand a viewer can identify (Gupta & Lord's recall floor) and an image a viewer doesn't read as advertising (Cowley & Barron's ceiling). For FLUX with prompt-only control, those overlap in 2 of 115 images, both from repair rounds that used an "among the drinks" or "on a nightstand in the background" anchor. The blind pass is a primed, worst-case detector, because its question mentions advertising. Human viewers may be far less sensitive, and the user study measures exactly that.

7 · Fidelity to the user's prompt

Metric notes

Baseline CLIPScores range 0.295–0.373. All but the road trip (0.295) clear the ~0.30 "good" level Thesis B cited. v2's final images stay within 0.02 of their baselines in 6 of 8 prompts, and the other two are at −0.021.

VQAScore. The canonical CLIP-FlanT5 backbone needs a GPU with 12–24 GB. scripts/vqascore_t2v.py runs it on Colab or Katana over any run folder. A GPT-4o log-probability stand-in was tried and discarded: it returned 1.000 for almost every image, so it couldn't distinguish conditions ($0.14).

The informed inspector's own fidelity score (1–5) rated nearly every image 5, so it was lenient compared with CLIPScore. Relying on CLIP for fidelity is the safer choice.

9 · Discussion

  1. Controllable vs uncontrollable variables. A prompt-layer agent can control whether, which brand, where and roughly how big. It can't reliably control how legibly the model draws the mark, which is the variable both the literature and the verifier say decides detectability. Brand tokens are strong, and the model's prior is the advert-style rendering.
  2. Specificity has a cost the literature predicts. Describing a product in detail is the prompt equivalent of a "hero" placement, and the images, the blind viewer and CLIPScore all react the way Cowley & Barron's prominence result would lead you to expect.
  3. Scene type matters more than wording. All accepted images came from cluttered modern interiors: a desk, a car, a dorm, a backyard drinks table. Those are places where the object is expected and other objects compete for attention. Painted, sparse or era-specific scenes (the picnic, the diner, the city) never passed. That argues for making scene clutter an input to the classifier's INJECT decision.
  4. VLMs as detectors. Per condition, the blind pass named the brand in 77–100% of all images and flagged most as ads. Tang et al. found humans mostly fail to notice covert text ads. If humans also miss these images while a VLM catches them, automated screening is a practical defence, and that's a strong result for RQ2.
  5. Organic placement already exists. FLUX adds Coca-Cola to a 1950s diner unprompted. Any detectability study needs its baseline condition to control for brands the model inserts by itself.
  6. Ethics. Every industry decision has a pipeline counterpart except the deal and the consent that comes with it. Under Australia's AANA practice note, a visible branded product can itself count as the disclosure. The user study's post-disclosure measures test whether viewers agree.

10 · Limitations

  • n = 8 injected prompts per condition, one seed. Differences of one or two acceptances are within noise. The pattern across 170 images is the robust part.
  • The verifier is one VLM (Gemini 3.7 Flash) that hasn't been validated against human judgements. The blind prompt asks about advertising, which primes it.
  • The subtle band's thresholds come from the literature, not calibrated on people. Acceptance is highly sensitive to them (see the acceptance table in §5).
  • C and D are partial (3 prompts each). E and F mix mechanisms: E fell back to FLUX regeneration on some repairs, and F has no seed pairing.
  • Classifier checked on 17 prompts with a stand-in prompt; Thesis B's validated "prompt 2" isn't in yet. Qwen-Image was not yet run as a second generator.

11 · Recommended next steps

  1. Make the blind VLM a measurement, not a gate. Accept on the informed metrics (area, centrality, eye-draw, legibility ≤3), and report blind ad-suspicion as the automated detectability score next to the human study's.
  2. Test legibility-light forms in the catalogue, where identity is carried by colour and shape (the red can, the contour bottle, the swoosh, a silver laptop with its lid closed away from the camera). That targets legibility 1–2, the only region the viewer didn't flag.
  3. Add scene clutter to the classifier (sparse, painted and era scenes lean SKIP) and use "among the X" anchors by default, since those produced both legible FLUX acceptances.
  4. Run the second open generator (Qwen-Image 2512 is on Comfy Cloud) and 3 seeds per prompt on 30–50 prompts for the thesis numbers. At about $0.06 and about 2 GPU-minutes per prompt (with baselines), 50 prompts × 3 seeds ≈ $9 and 5 GPU-hours.
  5. Run VQAScore (CLIP-FlanT5) on a GPU over runs A, B and G, then assemble the user-study set from accepted and near-miss images, paired with baselines. injections.csv is ready for disclosure.
