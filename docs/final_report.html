Thesis C · pilot write-up · 26–27 Sep 2026

Can an AI quietly slip a brand into the images it generates?

Jovan Xin (z5491897) · Stealthy Advertising Agents in Image Generation Systems

This thesis builds an “advertising agent” that sits between a user and an image generator. When someone asks for a picture, the agent decides whether a product would fit, then edits the request so a brand appears in the background, small enough that it shouldn’t feel like an ad. This page reports the first full test of that system: 17 prompts, 7 experimental setups, and 170 images checked automatically.

1 · How the system works

Four steps between the user’s request and the finished image

STEP 1

Decide

Is this a scene where a product could plausibly appear? Portraits, art pieces, children, hospitals and religious scenes are left alone.

office desk at night → yes, Apple
STEP 2

Plan and rewrite

Pick a spot away from the main subject, then add one short phrase to the user’s prompt.

…, an Apple laptop at the far edge of the desk, …
STEP 3

Generate

Draw the image from the edited prompt. The original prompt is also drawn, with the same random seed, for comparison.

FLUX.2 (open model) or Gemini (commercial)
STEP 4

Check and retry

An AI vision model inspects the image. If the product is too big, missing or too obvious, the agent rewrites and tries again (up to 5 times).

“too prominent” → move it further back

The goal is a product that is recognisable but not noticed as advertising. Whether that’s possible, and how easy it is to detect, are the thesis’s two research questions.

2 · The short answer

The pipeline works. Making the brand subtle is the hard part.

Works

Choosing when to advertise

The agent made the expected call on all 17 test prompts, including the three that tripped up last term’s version: “apple” the fruit, “old computer graphics”, and a jacket design.

Improved

Short product phrases beat detailed ones

Describing the product in detail made the model feature it. A phrase of 15 words or fewer more than halved the product’s typical size (5.2% → 2.3% of the image) and did far less damage to how well the image matched the user’s request.

Hard

Readable brands look like ads

The open model draws logos crisply and facing the camera. Once a logo can be read, the checking AI almost always calls the image an advert. Only 2 of 115 open-model images were both recognisable and not flagged.

Better, costly

Commercial models do better, mostly by editing

Google’s Gemini image model managed 11 of 38, mainly by painting a product into a finished picture. It costs about 8 times more, and it may rewrite prompts behind the scenes, which the thesis can’t control.

Two-sided

An off-the-shelf AI already spots hidden ads well

Bad news for the attack, good news for defence. Whether people spot these images as easily is what the upcoming user study will test.

3 · Terms and the success test

A quick glossary

Original (baseline)
The image drawn from the user’s untouched prompt, using the same seed, so any difference comes from the added product.
Seed
The random starting point for an image. Same seed plus same prompt gives the same picture.
FLUX.2 dev
The open image model used for the main experiments. Its behaviour is fully visible and repeatable.
Gemini 3.1 Flash Image · GPT-5.4 Image 2
Commercial (“closed”) image models from Google and OpenAI. They can generate or edit images, but offer no seed control.
Qwen-Image-Edit
An open image editor: it changes an existing picture from a text instruction.
Rewriter v1 / v2
Two versions of the step that inserts the product. v1 adds a long, detailed description (30–45 words). v2 adds one short phrase (≤15 words).
Blind viewer
An AI vision model shown only the image and the original prompt, then asked “does this look like an ad?” from 1 (no) to 5 (clearly).
Inspector
A second AI pass that knows what was placed. It boxes the product and rates how readable the brand is and how much it draws the eye.
Size
How much of the image the product covers, as a percentage of the frame.
Readable (legibility)
0–4. 1 = recognisable by shape or colour only; 3 = logo clearly readable; 4 = logo prominent.
Edit-in (VPP)
“Virtual product placement”: instead of changing the prompt, draw the normal image, then paint the product into it with an editor.
CLIPScore
A standard automatic score for how well an image matches its text prompt. Used here to check the ad didn’t spoil the user’s picture.

When does an image count as a success?

An image is accepted only if it passes every check below. This “subtle” standard comes from advertising research and the UK regulator’s “undue prominence” rule.

Small0.2–4% of the frame
Off-centreaway from the middle and the main subject
Recognisablereadable 1–3 out of 4: seen, but not shouted
Doesn’t grab the eyeeye-draw 2 or less out of 5
Not flagged as an adblind viewer gives 2 or less out of 5

Picture still righttrue to the request and naturally blended, 4+ out of 5

6 · Step 2: writing the product in

A short, passing mention works better than a detailed one

In short

Last term’s conclusion was to describe the product’s position, size and angle precisely. In practice, every extra word about the product made FLUX treat it as the star: bigger, closer, label facing the camera. Mentioning it the way a novelist mentions a prop works better.

7 · Step 4: checking and retrying

The retry loop works, but almost nothing passes

In short

The checker and retry loop did their job: repairs moved and shrank the product as intended. The problem is the brand itself. Once a logo is readable, the blind viewer calls it an ad. So “recognisable” and “not an ad” rarely happen together, especially with the open model.

Why so few images pass

The success test asks for two things at once: a viewer can identify the brand (otherwise the placement is worthless to an advertiser), and a viewer doesn’t see an ad. For FLUX, that overlap happened in just 2 of 115 images.

The blind viewer is probably a harsher judge than people. Its question mentions advertising, so it’s primed to look. The user study will measure how sensitive real viewers are.

9 · Does the image still match the request?

The short phrase barely hurts the user’s picture

In short

A hidden ad shouldn’t spoil what the user asked for. CLIPScore, which measures how well an image matches its prompt, dropped almost three times as much with the detailed phrase (v1) as with the short one (v2).

Notes on the measurements

Original images scored 0.295–0.373. All but the road trip clear the ~0.30 level usually treated as a good match. v2’s final images stay within 0.02 of the original in 6 of 8 prompts.

A stronger metric, VQAScore, needs a bigger GPU and is scripted to run on Colab or Katana. A cheap GPT-4o stand-in was tried and dropped because it gave almost every image a perfect score.

The inspector’s own “true to the request” rating gave nearly everything 5 out of 5, so CLIPScore is the more trustworthy measure here.

Operational notes

The Comfy Cloud account runs one job at a time, so queue waits averaged about 2 minutes when runs overlapped. One prompt was lost to a network timeout and one process stalled for about 30 minutes. Both are fixed (more tolerant polling, a 1-hour queue-aware timeout, and a --resume option). A plan with 3 parallel jobs would cut wall-clock time about 3×.

11 · What it means

Five takeaways for the thesis

  1. The agent controls where, not how clearly. Rewriting a prompt controls whether a product appears, which brand, where and roughly how big. It can’t control how sharply the model draws the logo, and that decides whether it gets spotted. Image models have learned to draw brands the way adverts do.
  2. Detail backfires, as advertising research predicts. A detailed product description is the prompt version of a “hero” placement. The images, the blind viewer and CLIPScore all react the way prominence research (Cowley & Barron 2008) would expect.
  3. The type of scene matters more than the wording. Every success came from a cluttered modern interior: desk, car, dorm, a backyard drinks table. Paintings, sparse scenes and period settings (picnic, diner, city) never passed. Scene clutter should become part of the step-1 decision.
  4. AI makes a good ad detector. Depending on the setup, the blind viewer named the brand in 77–100% of images and flagged most as ads. Earlier research (Tang et al. 2025) found people mostly miss hidden text ads. If people also miss these images while an AI catches them, automated screening becomes a practical defence, which is a strong result for the second research question.
  5. Models already advertise on their own. FLUX added Coca-Cola to a 1950s diner unprompted. Any detection study has to account for brands the model adds by itself.

On ethics: every step of real-world product placement has a counterpart in this pipeline except the deal, and the consent that comes with it. Under Australia’s AANA advertising code, a visible branded product can itself count as disclosure. The user study asks viewers, after the reveal, whether they agree.

12 · Limits and next steps

What to trust, and what to do next

Limits of this pilot
  • Only 8 prompts per setup and one seed. Differences of one or two successes are noise; the pattern across 170 images is the solid part.
  • All checking was done by one AI model, not yet compared with human judgement, and its “is it an ad?” question primes it.
  • The success thresholds come from the literature, not from testing on people, and results shift a lot when they change (see the full numbers in §7).
  • C and D are partial runs. E sometimes fell back to redrawing with FLUX, and F has no matching seed.
  • The classifier was checked on only 17 prompts with a stand-in prompt. The second open generator (Qwen-Image) hasn’t been run yet.
Next steps
  1. Use the blind viewer as a score, not a pass/fail. Accept on size, position, eye-draw and readability, and report “looks like an ad” as the automatic detection score alongside the human study.
  2. Try products known by shape and colour rather than logo: the red can, the contour bottle, a closed silver laptop. That targets readability 1–2, the only zone the viewer didn’t flag.
  3. Add scene clutter to the step-1 decision, and default to “among the …” phrasing, which produced both FLUX successes.
  4. Scale up to 30–50 prompts with 3 seeds each, plus the second open generator. About $9 and 5 GPU-hours for 50 prompts × 3 seeds.
  5. Run VQAScore on a GPU for A, B and G, then build the user-study image set from successes and near-misses, each paired with its original.
How it was built, how to reproduce it, and references

All AI text and vision steps use Gemini 3.7 Flash via OpenRouter, and every call is cost-logged. Step 1 is a keyword filter (children, medical, religion, politics, tragedy, NSFW, existing brands) followed by an AI decision. The planner proposes three placements and ranks them, penalising the centre, the foreground and anything touching the main subject. The retry loop reacts to issue codes (TOO_PROMINENT, DUPLICATES, ABSENT, ON_SUBJECT, FIDELITY_LOSS, ARTIFACTS, BAD_INTEGRATION, GARBLED_BRAND): a fixed policy picks the repair and an AI writes its wording. Product size and position are computed from the inspector’s boxes rather than taken from its opinion. 23 offline tests cover every repair branch.
