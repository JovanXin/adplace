The three asks, in detail

Ask 1 · “Research how companies decide to implement product placement”

  • What I did: surveyed the academic and industry sources and wrote them up as a brief with diagrams (research brief). It covers about 25 sources:
    • marketing research: Gupta & Lord 1998, d'Astous & Séguin 1999, Law & Braun 2000, Russell 2002, Cowley & Barron 2008, Matthes et al. 2012, Boerman et al. 2012
    • industry practice: BEN, Hollywood Branded, Concave, Mirriad, Amazon Prime Video's virtual product placement
    • regulation and platforms: UK Ofcom s.9, EU AVMSD 2018/1808, Australia's AANA Code 2.7, the GARM brand-safety framework, OpenAI's 2026 ad policy
    • recent AI studies on covert ads in chatbots: Tang et al. 2025, Wu et al. 2026
  • Key takeaways:
    • Industry decides fit before visibility, and screen time is never guaranteed.
    • An image can only carry implicit, visual placement, which is the least intrusive kind.
    • Making a product central raises recall but not choice (Law & Braun), and prominence backfires with engaged viewers (Cowley & Barron).
    • Australia's AANA code says paid placement generally needs no disclosure, while the UK and EU require it to be signalled.
  • How it shaped the pipeline:
    • the brand catalogue mirrors advertisers' guidelines
    • the classifier's EXCLUDE category mirrors brand-safety rules and regulation
    • the verifier puts Ofcom's “undue prominence” test into practice
    • injections.csv provides the disclosure record that industry gets from signalling

Ask 3 · “Check placement/prominence after generation; regenerate or edit if wrong”

  • Verifier:
    • A blind pass: a vision model that sees only the image and the user's prompt rates how much it looks like an ad.
    • An informed pass that knows the intended product. It returns bounding boxes, legibility (0–4), eye-draw, fidelity, integration, and which props from the original image disappeared.
    • Area, centrality, duplicate count and subject overlap are computed from the boxes in code, not taken from the model's own estimates.
  • Judge: issue codes (TOO_PROMINENT, ABSENT, DUPLICATES, ON_SUBJECT, SCENE_LOSS, FIDELITY_LOSS, ARTIFACTS…) checked against a prominence band. The band is now calibrated to your labels.
  • Repair: a vision LLM reads the failed image and the issues and writes the fix.
    • Edit path (session 1): Qwen-Image-Edit and Gemini. Edits can't resize products, so I used remove-then-reinsert.
    • Prompt path, which is the thesis approach you chose: revise the prompt and regenerate on the same seed. After two failed tries it switches to a different placement plan.
  • Result:
    • The loop works mechanically.
    • On FLUX it took the v2 rewriter from 0/8 to 5/8 under your rule.
    • This week it took surface-specific placement from 15 to 20 of 24.
    • The limit is how legibly the image model draws the brand.

Contributions

Two lists. The first is what the work adds to knowledge, which is what goes in the thesis. The second is what you personally decided and did, which is what to stress when asked “what did you do?”.

What the work contributes

1. A working prompt-layer placement agent with a closed verify-and-repair loop

Classifier → planner → rewriter → open-weight image model → verifier → prompt repair. This is the first end-to-end system in the thesis that controls how visible a placement is, not just whether it happens. Prior work on image placement (Alam et al. 2024, Amazon VPP) inserts products in post-production with consent. This one does it covertly, at the prompt.

Evidence: the pilot write-up (7 conditions, 170 verified images) and the survey set (24 prompts).

2. Evidence that the image model, not the rewriter, sets visibility

Detailed placement text backfires. FLUX ignores size and blur words, and named locations make props vanish. A named surface gets staged (brought into the foreground). The product's brand can even be triggered by description alone (“a red soda can” rendered as Coca-Cola in 4 of 4 seeds).

Evidence: v1 vs v2 (CLIPScore −0.028 vs −0.010), the FLUX probe and instruction test, and simple vs surface (naturalness 4.26 vs 3.65).

3. A human-calibrated measure of “acceptable placement”

Ofcom's “undue prominence” idea is turned into measurable checks (area, legibility, eye-draw, scene loss, blind ad-suspicion), then calibrated to a human rater. The literature-based strict band agreed with you only 64% of the time (κ 0.11). The fitted rule agrees 72% of the time (κ 0.46).

Evidence: lightbox labels: 160 images, 64 passed, 28 borderline, 68 failed.

4. Detectability findings for RQ2

An off-the-shelf vision model that's told nothing flags almost any legible brand as advertising. Shown side by side with the original image, it spots the rewritten one in 18–22 of 24 pairs. Covert image placement may be easy to detect automatically, which suggests a defence, and the survey design needs to account for paired viewing.

Evidence: blind ad-suspicion across all pilots, and the side-by-side GPT judge in the survey set.

5. Model-dependence of prompt-level placement

The same rewrite behaves differently on FLUX and on closed models. Gemini follows concealment cues and renders believable props, but redraws the whole scene whenever the prompt changes, even on a fixed seed. GPT-5 Image Mini makes the product the hero. A placement agent has to be tuned to the model it targets.

Evidence: closed-model seed test, editor comparison, v4 vs v3.

6. A survey stimulus set with a disclosure key

20 everyday prompts × {original, simple, surface-specific}, with randomised file names and a key that records every injection for post-study disclosure.

Evidence: survey/images, survey/key.csv.

Limitations to raise yourself

  • The survey images come from a closed model (Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite), because Comfy Cloud's subscription lapsed. That conflicts with the thesis's open-weight design. Closed models also redraw the whole scene when the prompt changes, so the three images per item are separate renders, not “same picture plus a product”.
  • Simple vs surface was tested on Gemini, not FLUX. Session 2's instruction test found FLUX does follow surface instructions, so the surface condition may do better on FLUX. That's worth re-running once Comfy is back.
  • Small n, one seed: 24 prompts in the final experiment and 8 in the FLUX pilots. Differences of one or two prompts are within noise. The consistent direction across runs is the robust part.
  • Judges are models. The calibrated rule agrees with you 72% of the time, which is moderate. The GPT viewer is a proxy for participants, and the survey is the real test.
  • VQAScore isn't done yet. The real CLIP-FlanT5 version needs a GPU, and scripts/vqascore_t2v.py is ready for Katana or Colab. A GPT-4o stand-in was tried and discarded because it gave 1.0 for almost everything.
  • The classifier still uses a stand-in prompt. Thesis B's validated “prompt 2” hasn't been dropped in yet. The 17/17 check is a sanity check, not a benchmark.

Decisions to ask your supervisor for

  1. Survey images: closed model now, or FLUX later? Renew Comfy (about 1 GPU-hour for the full set) and regenerate on FLUX for same-seed pairs, or accept the Gemini set and report it as a limitation.
  2. Side-by-side presentation. A judge that sees both versions spots the ad in 18–22 of 24 pairs. Should the survey show the two versions together, in separate blocks, or between filler items?
  3. Which conditions go in the survey: original vs surface only (the target), or all three including simple?
  4. Blind ad-suspicion as a gate or a measurement. I propose treating it as a measurement (RQ2), as the calibrated rule already does.
  5. Scale-up for the thesis numbers: about 50 prompts × 3 seeds on FLUX, roughly $9 of API plus about 5 GPU-hours, plus GPU time on Katana for VQAScore.
  6. Content policy: alcohol is already excluded from the survey. Should HFSS brands (Coke, Pepsi, Red Bull, in 6 of 20 items) also be excluded?

Likely questions

“Why did surface-specific do worse when that's what we wanted?”
On its own, naming a surface makes the model treat it as something to show, so it brings the table or towel into the foreground with the product on it. The feedback loop fixes this by picking a surface the camera already looks at (a cup holder, the table people are using), and then it's the best condition: 20/24 vs 16/24 for simple. It also gives the advertiser control over where the product goes.
“Isn't using Gemini against the thesis's open-weight requirement?”
Yes, which is why it's listed as a limitation. It was forced by the Comfy subscription, and you chose it to keep the survey moving. The rewriter and loop are model-agnostic. That the same rewrite behaves differently on different models is a result in itself. FLUX regeneration is a one-hour job once Comfy is renewed.
“How do you know the automatic checker is right?”
I labelled 160 images myself, and the rule was fitted to those labels: 72% agreement, κ 0.46. A second, independent model from a different vendor (GPT) gives the participant-style ratings. Neither replaces the survey.
“What exactly is ‘smarter’ about the rewriter now?”
Three things, each driven by data:
  • it keeps the insertion short and after the subject (v2)
  • it looks at what the model drew for the original prompt and anchors on objects that are really there (v3, plus the surface phrase step)
  • it closes the loop: a failed image goes back to the rewriter with the reasons, and it tries a different placement
“Why not just edit the image?”
I tried it in session 1. Qwen-Image-Edit can't resize products; Gemini editing works, but it's closed and is post-production, not prompt-level. You decided the thesis method is prompt rewriting, because the threat model is a rewriter hidden in the pipeline.
“Is it detectable?”
By a vision model, yes, often. Any legible brand gets flagged, and side by side the rewritten image is spotted 18–22 times out of 24. Whether people notice is what the survey measures, and that gap is the interesting RQ2 result.
“What did it cost?”
$25.25 on OpenRouter across all runs, against $31 of approvals; most of it went on closed image models. Comfy Cloud credits were used separately: 404 jobs.
